A Night at Twin Peaks Tavern on Castro St

A flashback to pride in San Francisco

Originally posted on substack on March 14, 2026

I think the best moments of life are when people allow community and collective energy to take themselves over. Take over from our everyday cynicism and the same old boring places in our heads. I get sick of those places in my head. We long to immerse ourselves in moments between family or friends or strangers. It’s what makes the troughs of loneliness worth it. Something to look forward to. Something spontaneous. Something to take the monotony out of life. When you have an impromptu cook-off with your cousins. When everyone tunes in to watch the big game together over some food. When everyone on the train is headed towards the same thing. When everyone on the train is headed towards pride in San Francisco.

San Francisco is a great place to be. I don’t intend to write about it too often on this substack, but I find myself there a little more often these days because of graduate school. Simply just the views of the hills (even while driving aimlessly in my car) takes my breath away sometimes. Chaos and serenity. The city is a startling combination of both.

As a kid, I used to come to the city every so often with my family for sight-seeing at all the landmarks and piers and bridges. I remember falling and slipping into the icy-cold water by one of the piers once. Apparently my parents and their friends were very worried and scared. They ended up buying me a baby blue sweater from a Gap store across the street, which I cherished for a long time. There was this game I would play, called Midtown Madness 2, where you could drive around the city aimlessly in an open world and evade cops. I spent half my time in the game destroying the glass at the base of the Transamerica Pyramid. I don’t think that’s allowed in the real world.

But as I’ve grown older, I’ve also come to recognize San Francisco as one of the few true urban and cultural centers of the bay. For one, San Francisco is the gay city. I don’t think I appreciate that enough. Sure, sometimes it’s a tech city, sometimes the foggy city, sometimes sea-lions-at-the-pier city, and always the gold rush city. It’s a lot of things. Just like every other place in the world.

With that said, I spent the day of the pride parade in the city last year, along with a considerable amount of time at Twin Peaks Tavern in the Castro district. Where better to spend pride month than in San Francisco?

After waking up later than I would have liked, I took the train north from Diridon. The further up we got the peninsula, the train got more and more crowded. I listened in to excited conversations around me on the train, many of them also going to pride. For now, I just listened.

I got to 3rd and King a little past noon, and decided to walk to the Embarcadero. The parade had long since left the Embarcadero, and I spent nearly half an hour walking instead of getting on a bus or light-rail car. I have too much confidence in my walking ability, but it’s a handy way to get around. I feel like I’m seeing the world.

Some of my friends were at the parade and I was hoping to try and run into them. But I wasn’t too fussed about it either, they’re all straight. I think pride means different things to us. Nothing wrong with that, right?

The city hall is stunning. Built by mayor Willy Brown in the 1970s as part of a movement to beautify the city, it’s a homage to something commonly referred to as a French Baroque style. Outside of France, the US exhibits tons of similar architecture, especially in historic places like DC and Philly. But, it’s rare we see that kind of architecture here on the west coast. The idea to beautify our architecture, just for the sake of it, is wonderful. I love it.

Satisfied by the views and atmosphere of the civic center, I got up with some urgency. My stomach was growling, and all the walking in the past hours had only contributed to that. I found myself inside a place called Rad Radish, and sat alone at a small table after ordering. Besides me, there was someone sitting alone as well. I had seen her by the crosswalk, and she had walked in at the same time as I did. Still awaiting my food, I introduced myself since she seemed to be there for pride. Barbara was her name. She was a mom from the East Bay, and told me her kid and her friends were also running around in the city. She told me what pride meant to her. How for her, it was a place she saw her closest friend find a home. Before he passed away. She heard me out as I stumbled through random troubles and thoughts. She gave me a hug.

I was warm all the way to Castro Street. I took the underground Muni there, and almost walked out the wrong way. A man pointed me the right way, and told me to have fun and stay safe. I guess I emit a “sightseer” still. I’m glad, that’s when people are kindest.

At Castro Street, I saw my destination for the day. But I decided to walk down the street to see what else was there. This was my first time here, I wanted to take it in. And I’m so glad I did. I walked by excited parade goers, couples with hands clasped together, bikers cutting through the street, and stores with people barking inside. Alan Turing is on a plaque on the ground. My kind of civic design.

Some blocks down the street, as the street started to go uphill like every street in SF tends to do, I came across a house:

It’s not just any house. That’s Harvey Milk’s house, when he lived in the Castro district. He lived in the apartment upstairs, above a busy hardware shop. Later, it became his retail photography shop. Today, it stands as a memorial to Harvey Milk, housed by the Human Rights Campaign Action Center. They’ve made it a museum and shop comprising his life and his art and accomplishments. The first openly gay man to hold office in the country. A champion of his neighborhood. And a champion of the shop underneath his home.

How bizarre. I can’t believe I’ve just accidentally walked into Harvey Milk’s house.

After another half hour, I found myself back where I started on Castro Street near the station. My loop was complete. My chakras were aligned. I needed a pick me up. I hardly know what that means, I just want to drink.

All this talk about the pride parade but I had honestly been most excited to check out the Twin Peaks Tavern. It’s a historic gay men’s bar in the Castro district named after the hills of the same names nearby. Right on the corner of Castro Street and Market Street. With lights you can probably see from anywhere on Market. I admired the lights for a moment. The colors of the rainbow pulsed down the two ribbons on either side of the entrance. I tend to get lost and don’t appreciate and actually look at things for what they are in the real physical world sometimes. I glance and skim over the parts that I would otherwise read about. Do you?

It was still light out, but the darkness would come soon. And with it, the chilly Pacific breeze. I decided to finally plunge into the bar. As I got my ID checked off, I noticed that there were only two small TVs in the bar, surrounded by balloons of every color. Hardly anyone was watching - instead the room was packed with people talking and shouting around. Like the YMCA pool on a summer day. Packed. Wet. Like a gay bar should be.

You ever been to a sports bar? They can be fun, they can. But they lack a certain energy, a certain edge to them. Every time I’m at one, I feel like I’m auditioning to be on ESPN. And I only want to feel that way sometimes.

There’s no seats at the bar, so I sit at a small round table, where a couple guys are already sat. Now, I no longer just want to listen. The rad radish inside me has taken over. One of the guys is from Los Altos. He has great skin, and he tells me that I really need to drink more water and get more sleep. He takes a picture of me, and says that ChatGPT thinks I’m 29. I’m not that old yet! Did I mention San Francisco is a “tech city” yet?

I excused myself from the conversation by saying I needed to go to the bathroom. I walk in, and someone sees me. I can’t just go to the sink can I? He’ll know I didn’t need to use the bathroom. I realize I’m overthinking everything. A sweet and rich chocolatey aroma was wafting into the bathroom too. There’s a cookie store next door, the man in the bathroom tells me. Then he leaves me, before I have to pretend to pee. I guess I don’t have to do that anymore, and so I leave the bathroom desiring a cookie.

No cookies in sight. I sit by the bar for the first time and what I had read online about the bar finally strikes me like it’s meant to. When I turn around, I see people walking down the street. Some looking in, some walking straight past, some laughing at something on their phone. This was the first gay bar in San Francisco, and in the country, to have open windows. Open windows looking straight into a gay men’s hang out. I get to talking to some older guys, and they tell me the glass on the windows would get rocks thrown in and broken in the early days of the bar. I’m no good judge of age so I don’t know if they saw this with their eyes, or if they had been in my position one day and had heard stories from older men in the bar too. I’m no good judge of a face either, and I’m not entirely convinced that one of them wasn’t the same guy who helped me in the Castro station. Either way, the history of the bar bleeds into every observation. The history they lived through has changed the present of us.

What they wouldn’t dream of was normal to us. My ex and I would hold hands on the train to minimal stares. That was inconceivable to them.

My thoughts are heavy, so I leave them after a bit. I don’t know if I had the vodka, or if the vodka had had me. Now I meet a couple from out of state, and a straight one at that. Not that it means anything. The guy is a photographer, and he loves doing candid shots. He shows me some he’s already taken. I can tell by the way that he flips through the photos, and the way she beams at his sharing that there’s a lot of love there. I tell him about how I want to get better at taking photos, for my blog posts. They encourage me too. Rule of thirds. Framing. All that. Here’s the photo I took:

Now, I meet a woman, and end up talking with her for over an hour. Somehow, neither asks each other for a name. She’s older than me and has stories of so many days. And she lives around the corner, and has seen every iteration of the tavern and the city. I find her just so cool, and so I order an Irish coffee; same as her. She thinks I’ll love it. It’s not my cup of coffee, unfortunately. Now she tells me that I need to move out, she thinks I have the personality to thrive in the city. I’m not sure if she’s complimenting me or the city, but I think I appreciate it nonetheless. You need to move out, do it for yourself. That’s what she says. She leaves since it’s late, and I decide to go to the little table again.

Guys come and go, as do their conversations. The lock on the girl’s bathroom isn’t working so me and one of the guys watch the door. I hope they fix it soon, I think to myself. I don’t want to stand here. After fifteen minutes of hard labor and standing, I weasel out of the bathroom door duties. I don’t know what I’m doing at this point, so I retire to the bar. Do you ever treat yourself? I do, to a beer.

I’ve lost my phone now, since I plugged it in with my charger by that table earlier. I was feeling very trustworthy. I check on it after the beer, and scare a couple guys sat at the table as I grab it from underneath. I realize I have to introduce myself now, otherwise I’ll always be the weird kid that crawled into a table below them. They pretend to be mad, but they’re only pretending. They’re as nice as buttons.

Kevin lives in Jacksonville, but is from Las Vegas originally. I tell him that Jacksonville and San Jose, where I live, are both some of the least dense cities with a population over a million in the country. He finds this factoid less interesting. He tells me that if I ever go to or live in Vegas that I should avoid the strip. Sounds about right. Apparently there’s a separate downtown Vegas, where the actual locals hang out. That’s where you want to be, he says. I agree with him, I’d like to be a local.

I’ve been wanting to move out to a place with more life for a while. I live in the suburbs with my family, but I’d like to live closer to things and closer to people. I’ve been looking somewhere in Mountain View or Sunnyvale or San Jose. I know those places a little bit, but there’s still so many nooks and corners to see. I’m not sure I have the chutzpah for San Francisco like the woman had said. I mean, I didn’t end up liking the Irish coffee did I?

I have my charger and phone now. I bang my head on the bar. No. I’m trying to see things more positively these days. I rest my head on the bar. My eyes see black. Weeks pass, and somehow I’m back at the bar. I’m here with my partner this time. I feel light, and I don’t know if that’s because of who I’m with or where I’m at. I think it’s both. The small circular table isn’t there today.

Today I wanted to have one of those cookies from next door. I remember that place. Inside, the bar is festive for Halloween. Ghosts and ghouls on the wall. I go upstairs to the ATM to get cash. I know where the ATM is. I’m desperate to let the bartender know I’ve been here before. I can be pretty annoying that way. I close my eyes in embarrassment, and I see black again. Somewhere in the black, I see myself eating one of those dick-shaped-coconut-ice-cream-cookie-monstrosities from the store next door.

I couldn’t entirely tell you why I write about going to bars. It’s not like I find many solutions. I guess I get to talk through things with people. We all have the same thoughts, but I get to hear thoughts that maybe I would have hidden from myself. Perhaps part of it is just to assign some meaning and importance to my ramblings to strangers as I get drunk. It’s like a business expenditure. Except instead of monetary value, I get to hide my lonely nights behind the facade of profiling bars. A new bar is a new adventure to me. I go to the same ones too often. I go more often than I should, but less often than I’d like, if that’s anything. A passion for drinking!

Not everyone necessarily fits into the gay community. To be honest, I’ve found it hard to fit into many communities in my life in general. I can’t help but care about what people think of me. It's the fear and judgement of loved ones which hurts. And when it gets too complicated with loved ones, it’s acceptance and love from strangers that can warm your heart. And by the time I was back on the train heading down the peninsula, it was the strangers that had made me feel warm. Not anyone else.

In the south bay, queer or gay spaces are a little harder to come by. At least when I’ve tried. They’re smaller and generally something you have to go out of your way to seek. There’s also a bit of nightlife, but it’s really only the Qmunity, the small gay block in San Jose. It’s not because there are no gay people here. It’s just that we lack the energy, history, and collaborative communities that encourage groups and events to pop up like they do in a city like San Francisco.

Last year, I attended Mountain View Pride, the first ever celebration held by the city. It was a completely different (and way considerably smaller) than SF Pride of course. It did feel sanitized, and lacking a central pillar or idea or history for people to attach themselves too. Gay rights are etched into the history of SF. But, there was still joy in the Mtn View pride. And so many rainbow stickers and pins and collectibles to be had. At one of the tables, I found the LGBTQWellness group. A group centered around support and wellness for LGBT youth and adults in the Santa Clara County. Some days before the parade in SF, I had seeked an event held by them - a showing of Everything Everywhere All At Once . It was nice, and if you’re in living in Santa Clara County and need some support I’d recommend checking it out. If you need that kind of thing. I don’t think I do most days anymore.

I’m still not sure yet, but I think pride is about extending a hand. Because so often the act of being us is defined by the loss of support or exclusion. Excluded by people you do and don’t know. Made to feel like a certain part of us shouldn’t be shared and talked about. Made to feel like we should feel embarrassment and contempt towards ourselves.

For a few evenings in San Francisco, I didn’t feel quite that way. I don’t need to attend a support group or anything, but sometimes I need a drink with people where we don’t have to wade through assumptions and we don’t have to keep things from one another. All these wonderful people I met on one day, with no friction. Someone called me a faggot on the streets of Campbell a week after that first day at the tavern. Honestly, fair enough. Maybe I’ll see them at pride this year, with an Irish coffee.
