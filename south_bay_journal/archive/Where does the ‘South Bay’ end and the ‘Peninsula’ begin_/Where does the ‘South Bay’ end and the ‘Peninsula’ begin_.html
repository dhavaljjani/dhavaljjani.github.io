The innate human desire for groupings + the next pub golf coming up!

Originally posted on substack on October 16, 2026

Imagine yourself as a migrant farm worker who has ended up in the Santa Clara Valley in 1915. In the Valley of the Heart’s Delight. The work is tough, but the cooler weather and lack of humidity has made you a fan already. It’s tough work but it’s nicer than the work by Fresno and further south. The country truly is booming. Your family shares a small ranch house in Santa Clara with a few others, but the great weather makes up for the somewhat cramped space. Plus, you love the fact that there are so many different towns around you. If you take a small trip you can end up in San Jose, Palo Alto, Stockton, Los Gatos, San Francisco, Santa Cruz, and so on and on. Every town has a slightly different character, but makes the area feel as bustling and alive as ever. Every time you go to a new town, you encounter a large new group of immigrants, maybe from El Salvador, maybe from Guatemala, maybe from Punjab, maybe from the Phillipines. Different industries make up every town. The quarries, the farms, the lumber, the government. It’s all here. You’ve heard all about the pomp of New York and St Louis and Chicago in film, but you’re convinced it can’t be anything like the varied and languid bayside dream you’re living here.

Today, the cities and towns in the bay have largely “globbed” together. Municipal and county boundaries can be completely missed entirely when crossing them. Because these boundaries are hardly “real”. Food of every kind is found in every town. Different library systems feel very similar. People travel between them all the time for work and pleasure. The streets look about the same. Signs hardly change. Industries are limited to a few. Strip malls dominate everywhere. Culturally, it can all feel very same-ish.

Yet, we idly argue online over who counts as being in the bay and who makes up NorCal, and what cities are part of what region in our never-ending Internet chatter. We use terms like north bay and east bay and peninsula and south bay. They must be something more than geographical groupings, right? How do we define them? I mean, where does one place end , and the next start? Where does, for example, the south bay start or end?

A recent obsession of mine is the Los Angeles metro area. I’ve oddly barely been, only having seen parts of Calabazas and Long Beach on a handful of days. My only other experiences with LA have to do with driving through the city to get to San Diego, awe-ing at the downtown cluster of buildings while passing through. It’s odd to me how little I know about LA considering I’ve been around so much of the rest of the state, including lots of remote areas. Maybe it’s my aversion as an Earthquakes fan or my innate desire to want to dislike LA as a Bay-Area-NorCal-Person.

But I cannot help but be continually enamored by LA and everything about it. The size of it, the music, the landscapes, the varieties. Just the sheer size of it alone. Los Angeles County is the largest county in the country by population, and that doesn’t even encompass the entire metro area. The counties of Malibu, Orange, Riverside, and San Bernardino also encroach upon the LA metro area.

A common question people in LA ponder deals with something similar; the very definition of Los Angeles. What really is Los Angeles? The city? The county? The metro area? Maybe it’s a state of mind? A lifestyle?

According to the Census, there are two separate MSAs (Metro Statistical Area) present there to make up one CSA (Combined Statistical Area). The Los Angeles Metro and the Inland Empire Metro. Where does the LA Metro end and the Inland Empire Metro start? Colloquially? There is no agreed upon definition. Obviously, Santa Monica is “in” LA, and Redlands is “in” the Inland Empire. But what about Diamond Bar? What about Pomona? Is Redlands also “in LA”, since LA trumps the Inland Empire by being the bigger metro? These are questions with no firm answers.

Here’s a particular way people in LA use their loosy-goosy definitions of areas. The San Fernando Valley, where the valley girl accent originates, is in a developed basin north of the Santa Monica Mountains. It is also largely within the municipal boundaries of the City of Los Angeles. Yet, you may encounter people in other municipalities, such as Santa Monica, telling locals from the SFV that they don’t really live in “LA”. Despite them being the ones living in a different city by definition. It’s clear that to most people, the term LA doesn't refer to the municipal government or city at all. Again, it’s a cultural or in-group based thing.

Across the country, in the New York metro, there’s a similar way their regional or geographical groupings are used. The term “bridge and tunnel people” is pompously used by Manhattanites to refer to people coming into the island for fun times, maybe from New Jersey or Brooklyn. These attitudes aren’t exclusive to the east coast. Here in the bay, a similar dynamic has existed. In the mid 90s, it was common for San Franciscans to refer to people coming into the city from Oakland/East Bay to work and to party as “nickels and dimes”. This is in reference to the area code in the east bay of course, 510.

It’s incredible the amount of unique ways us humans manage to be snobby. I guess it’s no different to the common phrase of “flyover state”. But at the same time, how odd is it that we can feel these feelings of “us” and “them” over something so shallow? I mean, the average depth of the San Francisco Bay separating San Francisco and Oakland is barely 20 feet…

Defining the South Bay

Given all these nebulously confusing thoughts, I wanted to try and “define” the South Bay as a region, if possible. San Jose is the heart of the South Bay in a sense, but how far does it go from there? When do towns go from being San Jose ‘burbs to San Francisco or Oakland suburbs? A naive first thought is that the South Bay is equivalent to Santa Clara County.

But, the county stretches far beyond our urban area that I consider. Most of the Diablo Ranges, almost down to San Luis Reservoir, and including most of the South County. I hear the term South County used for areas south of San Jose such as Morgan Hill, Gilroy, and San Martin. That area has much more agricultural ties still, and tends to be hotter and somewhat disconnected from life in San Jose. We need another definition.

Nickels and dimes bring me to the next way. Area codes! They provide a better jumping off point than the county. When we consider the south bay, the area code is 408 (and 669 after they ran out of 408 numbers). Area codes are managed and created by the North American Numbering Plan Administrator. However, despite how hard some people rep their area codes, they are solely reliant on local telecommunication carriers and where they can provide their services, rather than any regional reason.

The next urge is to try and use zip codes, but there are far too many zip codes in the South Bay to provide any regional context meaningfully. We can instead consider a definition tied to a local organization, such as the Valley Transportation Authority. Using an arbitrary definition of the South Bay being anywhere within a mile of a VTA light rail or VTA bus stop; I got this.

While all these definitions are good, none of them do a good job of showcasing the fact that a major part of what limits this area to…this area, is the mountains. We are boxed in by the bay to the north, and by mountains to the east, west, and most of the south. This is when we may want to try and consider more of a geomorphological definition. There’s another way to define a region. Possibly the most depressing way. Commuting patterns can confirm the places that people travel between most often, for both work and school. For better or worse, commutes are an integral part of our lives, and proximity to work best defines what urban centers we live in. However, in Northern California, we live in this large interconnected web. People commute in and out of their local areas increasingly more as cars and remote work enable us. We are not restricted to having to live in the same areas we work in. So many people live in the city and work in the South Bay, and vice versa. Amtrak and CalTrain thankfully enable this.

At this point, getting mostly nowhere, I started to ask some other locals and random people in an attempt to crowd source the answer instead. Despite my small and unrepresentative sample size, I picked up some interesting tendencies. People defined the South Bay in terms of cities mostly. Everyone agreed Milpitas was South Bay. No one agreed with the sentiment that Morgan Hill or Palo Alto were South Bay. Mountain View and Los Altos were considered so, most of the time. And Los Altos Hills is more likely mid-peninsula or peninsula to the people I asked. Fremont is undoubtedly East Bay.

The truth is that the south bay has no concrete definition. No region whose definition extends past a legal or administrative world does. For the south bay, the mid-peninsula/peninsula takes over on the west at one point, and the east bay by the border of Milpitas and Fremont. Towards the south it’s a little clearer, but still, who’s to say? All of this isn’t even considering the term “Silicon Valley”, which is a whole other conversation. That term darts between real-world geographical definitions and industry focused definitions. Mark Zuckerberg is quintessentially Silicon Valley. Gary Singh is quintessentially South Bay. Neither person fits the other term.

It’s enjoyable to debate the measure of these regions and terminologies and talk about what neighborhoods are and aren’t part of what area, and if people from Walnut Creek are valid, and where the mid-peninsula is, and if Tracy is in the bay, and so on and so on. It’s fun talking, it’s what we do as humans.

These debates arise because, in the USA, regional identity and culture can be somewhat homogenous, making our distinctions appear arbitrary. It’s not entirely distance that defines when things change. Some parts of the country appear wildly different to one another , while some places are hundreds of miles apart and mostly just the same. Even though they’re a country apart, it would not be totally wrong to consider SF and NYC as more “similar” than say, San Francisco and the city of Needles in California, which rests in the Mojave Desert some 2100 miles closer to San Francisco than New York. Obviously you say, the first two share some traits and culture in their populations due to being diverse, large, and worldly cosmopolitan cities. Especially in our modern and globally connected world. Needles is in California just like San Francisco, but that’s about where the similarities end. I drove through there once, and I guess both cities also seem to like their weed a good bit too.

Sometimes on this blog though, I worry I stray too far from the south bay as a region and topic. But then I remember that the south bay encompasses the world, just as the world encompasses the south bay. Is writing about Fremont really that unrelated? Are the mountains of the Santa Cruz really that disjoint from our daily lives here? They impact our weather, at the very least. The Chinese restaurants of Saratoga exist here as something larger than solely this region, of course they do. Who made them a thing??

The truth is that all places are incredibly interconnected and defining locations as disjoint areas is virtually impossible. Tobler’s law of geography sums it up; “everything is related to everything else, but near things are more related than distant things.” Duh!
